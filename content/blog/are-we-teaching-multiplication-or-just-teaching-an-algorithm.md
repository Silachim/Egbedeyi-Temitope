It is no secret that many students in the United States struggle with learning multiplication, a difficulty that has been widely documented in research literature. This opinion is not about what goes wrong or right in the classroom, but about bringing perspective to students' exposure to different multiplication algorithms.
Flipping through the pages of research literature, you will read about how important multiplication is to other components of upper-elementary mathematics. Multiplication requires complex mathematical skills, including students' ability to coordinate place value, number decomposition, and multiplicative reasoning (Egbedeyi & Kosko, in press; Carpenter et al., 1996; Young-Loveridge & Mills, 2009). Due to its importance, different algorithms have been developed to solve multiplication problems, such as the Standard Algorithm, non-standard algorithms like the Box/Area Model, the Partial Products Algorithm, the Lattice Method, and others. These different approaches provide opportunities for students to solve multiplication problems in different ways.
On October 13, I was privileged to present a research brief alongside my advisor, Professor Karl W. Kosko, on research funded by the National Science Foundation (NSF). Professor Kosko is the principal investigator of the NSF-funded project, but his willingness to allow a research assistant to lead an investigation within the grant deserves to be noted and documented. He gave me the privilege of leading the investigation examining the multiplication algorithms used by fifth- and sixth-grade students.
While this post is not meant to duplicate the research results, it is necessary to discuss some of the findings in a way that helps teachers understand them, especially elementary school teachers in Nigeria.
The findings showed that, among fifth-grade students, 19.7% used the Box Model, 8.1% used the Partial Products Algorithm, and 72.2% used the Standard Algorithm. Among sixth-grade students, 31.1% used the Box Model, 3.6% used the Partial Products Algorithm, and 65.3% used the Standard Algorithm.
These findings suggest that while the Standard Algorithm was the most commonly used approach among fifth- and sixth-grade students, a larger proportion of sixth-grade students used the Box Model than fifth-grade students.
![Multiplication algorithm use among fifth- and sixth-grade students](/assets/images/blog/multiplication-algorithms/algorithm-use-grades-5-6.png)

*Figure 1. Distribution of multiplication algorithms used by fifth- and sixth-grade students.*
Also, Egbedeyi and Kosko (2025) reported in their study of fourth-grade students that the Standard Algorithm was the most frequently used strategy, followed by the Box Model and the Partial Products Algorithm, while repeated addition and doubling strategies were infrequently used.
With results from these two studies by Egbedeyi and Kosko (2025, 2026), the most pressing question is: Which algorithm is best?
Before we answer that question, it is important to recognize that the most frequently used algorithm is not necessarily the best for every child. The frequency of use alone does not tell us whether students understand the mathematical reasoning behind the algorithm.
This article is not here to debate whether teachers should favor one algorithm over others, but to show teachers the various algorithms available for teaching multidigit multiplication and to encourage greater attention to students' multiplicative reasoning. A future post will address the relationship between multiplicative reasoning and multiplication.
Let us return to the purpose of this article.
Consider a simple multiplication problem: 23 × 14.
![Standard Algorithm](/assets/images/blog/multiplication-algorithms/standard-algorithm.png)

*Figure 2. Standard Algorithm for 23 × 14.*
Using the Standard Algorithm, a student multiplies 23 by 4 to obtain 92 and then multiplies 23 by 10 to obtain 230. Adding the two partial products gives 322.

*Figure 3. Partial Products Algorithm for 23 × 14.*
Using the Partial Products Algorithm, another student decomposes 23 into 20 and 3, and 14 into 10 and 4. The student then calculates 20 × 10 = 200, 20 × 4 = 80, 3 × 10 = 30, and 3 × 4 = 12. Adding these four products gives 322.
![Partial Products Algorithm](/assets/images/blog/multiplication-algorithms/partial-products-algorithm.png)
Using the Box/Area Model, a third student represents the same four partial products in a rectangular arrangement before adding them to obtain 322.

![Box/Area Model](/assets/images/blog/multiplication-algorithms/box-area-model.png)

*Figure 4. Box/Area Model for 23 × 14.*
All three students arrive at the same answer, but they use different approaches. 

Interestingly, the algorithms are connected through place value, number decomposition, and the distributive property. This raises another question: If children can solve the same multiplication problem using different algorithms, why should their exposure be limited to one?

Some countries around the world, such as Nigeria, appear to favor the Standard Algorithm in classroom instruction. In Nigeria, the Standard Algorithm remains a familiar approach to teaching multidigit multiplication. The recognition of the Standard Algorithm has systematically made it almost the only, or let me say the most common, algorithm children are exposed to when learning multidigit multiplication.

This is despite the fact that many primary school mathematics textbooks include other methods of solving multiplication problems. Yet some of these methods are presented in ways that still emphasize procedures rather than helping children understand the mathematical relationships behind them.
The overreliance on the Standard Algorithm raises questions: Is the Standard Algorithm the only method that works for Nigerian students? Are we interested only in procedural knowledge instead of both conceptual and procedural knowledge? Have some teachers become so comfortable with the Standard Algorithm that they are unwilling to explore or teach other algorithms? Are Nigerian students so dull that they cannot learn a new algorithm for multiplication? All these questions deserve answers, but who is ready to answer them?

I am not a master of ranting, but a concerned person who is looking toward the educational future of an ordinary Nigerian child. My concern is not that the Standard Algorithm is bad or should be abandoned. Far from it! The Standard Algorithm has its place in mathematics instruction, and students should learn to use it accurately and efficiently. However, its usefulness should not prevent teachers from exposing children to other algorithms that could help them understand multiplication differently.

Neither am I suggesting that teachers should introduce every available algorithm to students. Teaching several algorithms without helping children understand how they work may simply create another problem. What matters is that children have opportunities to explore different approaches, explain their reasoning, and understand the mathematical relationships connecting those approaches.
For the educational future of a Nigerian child to be preserved, mathematics must be given its rightful place, not just as a compulsory subject required for admission to tertiary institutions, but as an empowerment tool to arm children with critical thinking and advanced multiplicative reasoning.

I call on teachers and other educational stakeholders in Nigeria to teach and learn different multiplication algorithms, introduce them to students, and let children explore and choose the approach that suits them when solving multiplication problems, especially multidigit multiplication. After all, if the purpose of teaching mathematics is to develop children's mathematical thinking, why should we be satisfied with teaching them only one way to arrive at an answer?
