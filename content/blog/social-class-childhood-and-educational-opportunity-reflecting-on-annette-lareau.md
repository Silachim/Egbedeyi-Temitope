***Author's Note***: *I originally wrote this reflection in 2023 in response to Annette Lareau's scholarship on social class, childhood, and inequality. I have lightly edited it for publication on this website while preserving the substance of my original argument.*

Lareau is known for her work titled *Unequal Childhoods: Class, Race, and Family Life.* *Unequal Childhoods* examines differences in childhood experiences, including those associated with social class and race. She acknowledged that the relationship between social inequality and social class could be traced to the significant differences between the childhood experiences of African American and white children; that is, social inequality among different societal classes contributes significantly to the education and general well-being of children in the United States (Lareau, 2015). 

Lareau, throughout her works, continues to show how social inequality, although invisible, is contributing to the difference in the educational attainment of children in the United States (Lareau, 2002). The crux of Lareau's work is that social inequality affects important aspects of people's everyday lives. For instance, she narrated that social inequality influenced the organization of daily life, language usage, and social connection among working-class or poor families and middle-class families. 

I find it difficult to disagree with her position about the impact of social inequality on children's education because evidence from developed and developing nations like Nigeria shows the gulf between children from wealthy and poor families. Many children from working-class or poor families in Nigeria do not have access to quality education, unlike children from wealthy families who have greater opportunities to receive quality education within and outside Nigeria. Just like in Nigeria, research has documented educational inequalities affecting children of color in the United States (Darling-Hammond, 2007; Morgan, 2020). Although Lareau tried to state that social class does not affect every aspect of family life, I was expecting Lareau to state those aspects that are not influenced by social class; however, it is expedient to hint that there is variability within the class, which means even within a social class, there is still a class (Lareau, 2002, p. 772). 

Likewise, Lareau's connection between social inequality and parenting suggests that many parents raise or train their children based on their social class. The social class of parents was categorized into two categories: working-class/poor families and middle-class families. According to Lareau's views, working-class/poor families used natural growth to train their children. My interpretation is that this may be partly related to limited opportunities available for their children to utilize. In contrast, middle-class parents use concerted cultivation to prepare their children for the future, which means they make an effort to provide opportunities that will sustain their social class. This classification of the parent social class seems to be lacking another class, which is rich or influential families. My belief is that the latter class (influential families) influences the extent of the economic power available to both working-class and middle-class families, which in turn influences the type of education and society available to children from these two social classes.

My conclusion based on the work of Lareau is that social class should not be an integral part of determining people's access to opportunities because available societal resources should benefit all members of society. Since current economic conditions show that members of society do not have equal access to limited resources, achieving complete social equality may remain more of an aspiration than a reality. Instead of focusing only on social equality, society must also promote equity because promoting equity will help every member of society feel appreciated and have meaningful access to basic rights, such as free and quality education for all children irrespective of their family background.
