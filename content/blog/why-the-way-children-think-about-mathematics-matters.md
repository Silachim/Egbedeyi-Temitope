There is a question adults often ask children after a mathematics problem: “What answer did you get?”

It is a reasonable question. But over the years, I have become much more interested in another one: “How did you get it?”

That small change in wording can open a window into a child’s thinking.

Give several children the same multiplication problem and you may see several different approaches. One child may write down the procedure taught in class. Another may break the numbers into smaller parts. Someone else may draw groups, make an array, count repeatedly, or invent a method that looks rather unusual at first.

They may even arrive at the same answer. But they have not necessarily done the same mathematics.

## There is a story behind the answer

Imagine asking a child to solve 24 × 6.

One child might immediately use the familiar written algorithm. Another might say, “I know 20 sixes are 120, and four more sixes are 24, so it is 144.”

A third child may draw six groups of 24.

If all three arrive at 144, it is tempting to put three check marks on the page and move on. Yet those solutions tell us different things about what each child sees in the problem.

That interests me.

I want to know what children notice when they encounter mathematics. What relationships do they see? Why does one strategy make sense to them while another does not? What happens when the numbers become larger or the problem is presented differently?

And sometimes the most interesting response is not the correct one.

## Wrong answers can be surprisingly useful

Anyone who has taught mathematics knows the familiar experience of looking at a child’s work and wondering, “How did they get that?”

Our first instinct may be to correct the mistake. Of course, children eventually need to know when an answer is incorrect. But before erasing it, there is value in understanding where it came from.

A wrong answer is not always random.

Sometimes a child has noticed a genuine mathematical pattern but used it where it does not apply. Sometimes the child understands one part of the problem but has lost track of another. Sometimes a procedure has been remembered without the idea behind it.

And occasionally, what initially looks like a strange solution turns out to be quite sensible once the child explains it.

That is one of the things I find fascinating about studying children’s mathematics. Their work can surprise us.

## Multiplication is bigger than the times table

Many of us first encountered multiplication through the times table. We memorized facts, recited them, were tested on them, and perhaps competed to see who could answer fastest.

There is nothing wrong with knowing multiplication facts. Fluency is useful.

But multiplication becomes much more interesting when children begin to understand the relationships underneath those facts.

- Why is 6 × 8 related to 3 × 8?
- What happens to a quantity when it is doubled?
- Why can 24 × 6 be thought about as 20 × 6 and 4 × 6?
- How does an array help us see multiplication differently?

These are not merely tricks for getting an answer. They are signs that a child is beginning to see how quantities are related.

Researchers often describe this kind of thinking as multiplicative reasoning. The phrase sounds technical, but the basic idea is quite ordinary: children are learning to think about groups, quantities, and how one amount relates to another.

And that way of thinking travels far beyond multiplication. It eventually matters when children encounter division, fractions, ratios, percentages, proportions, measurement, and algebra.

## The classroom matters too

Children do not develop these ideas alone.

Think about the difference between two mathematics classrooms.

In one, the teacher demonstrates a method, students practise it, answers are checked, and the class moves on.

In another, the teacher may still teach the method, but also asks questions such as “Who solved it another way?”, “Why does that work?”, “Do these two strategies mean the same thing?”, and “Can someone explain what she did?”

Suddenly, mathematics becomes a conversation.

Children are not simply producing answers. They are explaining, listening, comparing, disagreeing, defending an idea, changing their minds, and learning what counts as a convincing mathematical explanation.

Those moments are important. They are also part of why my research has increasingly looked not only at the strategies children use, but at the classroom conditions surrounding those strategies.

## Sometimes we need to look more closely

There is another challenge in studying children’s thinking: children cannot always tell us everything they noticed.

A child may look at one part of a mathematical representation before another. Something on the page may attract attention immediately. A child may spend considerable time examining a feature and barely look at something we assumed would be important.

That is one reason I became interested in using eye-tracking in mathematics education research.

Eye-tracking does not read a child’s mind. No technology can do that.

What it can do is give us another piece of evidence. Alongside children’s written work, explanations, gestures, and conversations, it can help us examine where their visual attention goes while they are working through a problem.

> For me, the technology is not the interesting part by itself. The child is.

The technology is useful only when it helps us ask better questions about learning.

## Perhaps we should ask one more question

When a child brings home a mathematics worksheet, it is natural to look at the marks.

How many were correct? Which ones were wrong? What was the score?

Those things have their place.

But every now and then, it may be worth choosing one problem and asking: “How did you think about this one?”

Then listen.

The explanation may be incomplete. It may take a while. It may involve fingers, drawings, invented language, or a strategy you would never have used yourself.

That is precisely why the conversation can be worthwhile.

Because behind the answer is a child trying to make sense of mathematics.

And if we want to understand how children learn, that thinking deserves our attention.
